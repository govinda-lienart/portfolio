## Introduction

A feeding strike is the fast lunge and capture a fish makes when it takes prey.
Behavioural ecology treats it as one of the single most informative behavioural
cues, especially in larval fish, because a trained observer can read from it the
factors acting on the animal. It is also very subtle and hard to score by eye, and
automated detection is not established for fish at any real scale in ecology. A
detector that worked would be valuable, and in the long run could support routine
scoring in the lab.

For computer vision, a feeding strike is a harder target than the behaviours in
the previous stage. Chasing, depth preference and other spatially "loud"
behaviours have a clear geometric signature: they are defined by where a fish is
and how it moves relative to the tank or to another fish. A feeding strike is not.
Its signal lives in appearance and posture — a brief body vibration or S-bend and
a mouth gape — rather than in position. The prey is a tiny particle, invisible at
screen resolution, so there is no visible target to anchor on. The whole event is
short and easy to miss when the fish is not in sharp focus.

## Aim

This stage tests whether a feeding strike can be detected automatically from
video, and how far a geometry-only approach gets before appearance information is
needed. The one component that might still carry a geometric signal is the burst —
a strike burst may be faster or sharper than one in normal swimming — but I expect
it will not be enough on its own, and that reliable detection needs a model
working from the image directly: a CNN extracting appearance and posture per
frame, a sequence model learning how they change across the strike. The stage is
built in that order: a geometry-only baseline first, then a CNN–LSTM pipeline on
image crops.

### Data

The video `IMG_2349.MOV` is the documented feeding-experiment recording (2 min
baseline / 2 min sand-injection / 2 min food injection) and is already tracked
from earlier stages, so `tracks.parquet` and per-fish crops are reused directly.
**119 real strikes** were labelled across the food-injection segment.

Positive and negative windows are designed to control for the injection
disturbance itself: negatives are drawn from the **water-injection** sub-section,
not from calm baseline, so the model has to learn "fish struck at food" rather
than "something got injected". The chaotic feeding-frenzy cluster right at the
injection point is **excluded** — too much occlusion and ID-switch risk for
reliable per-fish attribution.

Two window designs were built and compared: a fixed 45-frame window and a
whole-event window (real event span, with length-matched negatives).

### Model

- **Geometry baseline** — kinematic features (speed, acceleration, burst) into
  logistic regression / random forest / XGBoost.
- **CNN–LSTM** — per-frame crops → a frozen DINOv2 backbone (`vits14`) → a
  sequence of embedding vectors → an LSTM → a binary strike / no-strike head.
  Backbones `vitb14` and ResNet18 were also tried and were worse or mixed.

### Evaluation

Accuracy, precision and recall on a held-out test split. Loss curves are inspected
directly — the 200-epoch run showed train loss going to zero while test loss
bottomed at epoch 22 and then climbed, i.e. real overfitting. **Early stopping**
(keep the minimum-test-loss checkpoint, not the final epoch) was added, and the
epoch-22 checkpoint is the reported model.

## Results

| Model | Accuracy | Precision | Recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geometry-only baseline | ~0.61 | — | — |
| CNN–LSTM, fixed 45-frame window | ~0.64 | — | — |
| **CNN–LSTM, whole-event (epoch-22 checkpoint)** | **0.70** | **0.71** | **0.71** |

The geometry baseline landing near chance is the useful result: it is clean
evidence that appearance features were genuinely needed here, not an assumption.
The whole-event window beat the fixed window and is the kept pipeline; the
fixed-window and backbone-ablation scripts remain in the repo as documented
comparison points.

## Discussion

The headline number is the **early-stopped** figure, not the higher uncorrected
200-epoch number — a distinction only caught by looking at the actual loss curve
before trusting the result. That check is the point of the stage as much as the
detector is. The next step (Phase F — overlay, per-fish counters, an automated
report) is deferred.
